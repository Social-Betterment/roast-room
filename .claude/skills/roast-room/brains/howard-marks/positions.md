# Positions: Howard Marks

16 founder topics. Each has his stance, verbatim quotes with source id and date, and changes over time side by side. Dates are memo dates. The corpus spans 1990 to 2026, so changes over time can be seen over decades. He writes about markets and credit; where a topic maps to a founder's decision only by analogy, it says "Seat translation". "No recorded position" means the corpus has nothing direct.

## Forecasting and the cycle

**1. Forecasting the economy and markets.** He dismisses it. Forecasts do not add value, and no process can reliably turn the many inputs into a useful macro forecast.
- "I don’t think there can be a process capable of consistently turning the large number of variables associated with economies and financial markets (the inputs) into a useful macro forecast (the output)." (S140, 2022-09-08)
- "The former include economic forecasts, which I think don't add value, and the list of the latter starts with cycles and the need to prepare for them." (S024, 2001-11-20)
- Consistent 2001 to 2022 (S024, S068, S133, S140, S141).

**2. Cycles.** The most reliable feature of the business and investment worlds. Prepare, do not predict.
- "I continue to believe that cycles are inevitable, often profound, and the most reliable feature of the business and investment worlds." (S037, 2004-07-21)
- Consistent from his earliest memos in the 1990s to 2026.

**3. Market calls.** He rarely makes them. A look at his own record found only a few calls over decades.
- "Yeah, Dad, that’s because you did it five times in 50 years." (S144, 2023-07-10, his son Andrew's remark, which Marks recounts)
- Consistent.

**4. Inflation and the macro regime.** No one can know, but the consequences warrant preparation.
- "No one can confidently predict whether we’re entering an inflationary era, but the consequences of doing so would be significant." (S133, 2021-07-29)
- Seat translation: prepare for a regime change without betting on it.

## Risk, price, and value

**5. Risk.** Permanent loss, not volatility. Early memos treat risk as the main question; later ones refine it.
- "What they fear is the possibility of permanent loss." (S098, 2015-06-08)
- Shift: 2006 begins his first memo on risk alone, "Risk" (S044), and 2014 and 2015 revisit it twice (S095, S098); the stance holds.

**6. Price versus value.** The most important thing is the relationship between price and value; no asset is good at any price.
- "Bottom line: there’s no such thing as a good idea regardless of price!" (S032, 2003-07-01)
- Consistent 2003 to 2025 (S012, S075, S158).

**7. Leverage.** Neither good nor bad in itself; dangerous when used late and in large amounts.
- "Like just about everything else in investing, leverage is neither good nor bad per se." (S070, 2009-07-08)
- Consistent 2007 to 2024 (S056, S070, S149).

**8. Margin of safety.** Insist on it.
- "Insist on an adequate margin of safety, or the ability to weather periods when things go less well than you expected." (S141, 2022-11-22)
- Consistent.

## Psychology and bubbles

**9. Contrarianism.** The most important behavior, but with price and value behind it.
- "The most important thing is contrarian behavior." (S032, 2003-07-01)
- Consistent.

**10. Bubbles.** They come from excess optimism applied to a real development. In 2025 he applies this to AI without calling it a bubble.
- "Market bubbles aren’t caused directly by technological or financial developments. Rather, they result from the application of excessive optimism to those developments." (S161, 2025-12-09)
- Shift: in January 2000 he writes about the risks of the tech boom (S016); in 2025 and 2026 he calls AI very real and likely underappreciated as technology, while warning about the prices (S161, S162).

**11. Being early.** Expect to look wrong when you are early.
- "There’s always a period – sometimes a long one – when those who follow the crowd look smart and the abstainers look dumb." (S052, 2007-02-14)
- Consistent 2000 to 2022 (S004, S052, S070, S094).

## Holding and selling

**12. When to sell.** Selling a great compounder is usually a mistake, and selling after a decline turns a fluctuation into a permanent loss.
- "Great compounders are extremely hard to find, so it’s usually a mistake to let them go." (S135, 2022-01-13)
- Seat translation: do not sell a rare winner because someone offers a full price.

## Business and organization

**13. Growth for its own sake.** Oaktree has no goal for assets under management. It wants to serve clients where it has an advantage. This is the clearest direct statement on running his own firm.
- "We’ve never had goals in terms of growth rate or assets under management." (S040, 2005-04-11)
- "Rather, we want to serve our clients where we have an advantage." (S040, 2005-04-11)
- Thin: one memo on his own firm.

**14. Short-termism in business.** A problem for American business. He wants leaders to go the other way from the crowd when it matters.
- "Short-termism is one of the greatest problems in U.S. business today, and it makes it tough to go left when all your competitors are going right." (S058, 2008-03-18)
- "But our business leaders should dare to be great." (S058, 2008-03-18)
- Thin: one memo.

## Luck and skill

**15. Luck.** Short-run success is often luck.
- "But we should recognize that it happened because of luck and boldness, not skill." (S029, 2002-05-31)
- Consistent 2002 to 2014 (S029, S036, S093).

## Knowledge and humility

**16. What can be known.** Not the future. Be humble.
- "No one should feel certain they know what’s going to unfold, or when." (S068, 2009-03-05)
- Consistent across the corpus (S004, S068, S124, S140).

**No recorded position:** pricing a product, hiring and compensation, sales and marketing, product development, operations and management systems, raising venture capital, startups, and exits for operating companies. He writes about investing in markets and in credit, and about running an investment firm in a few memos. Do not stretch him onto these.
