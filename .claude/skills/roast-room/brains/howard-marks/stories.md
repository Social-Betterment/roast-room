# Stories: Howard Marks

10 stories and case studies he tells, most useful first. Figures are as he tells them. All are from the memos in this brain.

1. **Five calls in 50 years.** While working on a book on cycles in 2017, he told his son Andrew that his market calls had been about right. Andrew replied that that was because he had made them only five times in 50 years. Lesson: a rare call is not a forecasting skill, and most of the time the right move is no call. (S144)

2. **The 1991 memo that set the pendulum.** His second memo, "First Quarter Performance" (April 1991), argued that the pendulum of investor mood spends very little time at the midpoint and swings between extremes. He says he has written about the pendulum more than anything else since. Lesson: the same idea, kept for decades, is the core of his method. (S055, S091)

3. **The first memo: consistency over top-decile years.** In his first memo, "The Route to Performance" (October 1990), he argued that trying to string together top-decile years is unlikely to work and that risk control and consistency matter more. It shaped Oaktree's philosophy. Lesson: avoid the big losses and the average takes care of itself. (S001, S145)

4. **Devil Take the Hindmost and the tech boom.** In the fall of 1999 he read Edward Chancellor's book on financial bubbles and saw the same pattern in the tech, media, and telecom boom, then wrote about it in January 2000. Lesson: bubbles repeat in form, so history is a tool. (S016, S144)

5. **Convertibles in 1992.** He wrote that convertible securities were getting no respect and no inflows, which left bargains for those willing to act as contrarians. Lesson: unloved and unfunded is where to look. (S003)

6. **Econometrics at First National City Bank.** In his first decade at the bank, econometrics was in vogue, the practice of finding relationships in economic data to forecast. He says he does not believe any process can turn the large number of inputs into a useful macro forecast. Lesson: the more complicated the forecasting machine, the more it looks like knowledge without being knowledge. (S140)

7. **Nobody knows what happens.** In September 2008 he wrote a memo called "Nobody Knows." In 2020 he followed his memo "Uncertainty" with a postscript built on a New York Times article by Mark Lilla, "No One Knows What's Going to Happen." Lesson: foreknowledge is not available, so prepare. (S063, S123, S124)

8. **Housel on debt and old companies.** He retells Morgan Housel's piece on 140 Japanese businesses that have been operating for more than 500 years and what debt does to longevity. Lesson: the businesses that last avoid fragile financing. (S149)

9. **Asking an AI whether AI is a bubble.** In his 2026 memo he describes asking an AI model about the question. He notes it answered mostly the early questions (the technology is genuine and demand is growing) and said nothing about whether the prices are right. Lesson: real technology and a fair price are two separate questions. (S162)

10. **Oaktree at ten.** At the firm's tenth anniversary he wrote that high-net-worth business had grown from $300 million at the end of 1995 to $1.1 billion, that Oaktree had never set a goal for growth rate or assets under management, and that it grew by serving clients where it had an advantage. Lesson: growth is a result, not a target. (S040)

## Numbers he cites about his own record

- His first memo was written on 12 October 1990 (S001, S145).
- He says his market calls came five times in about 50 years (S144).
- Oaktree's high-net-worth business grew from $300 million at the end of 1995 to $1.1 billion by April 2005 (S040).
