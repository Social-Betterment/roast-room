# Frameworks: Philip Fisher

14 frameworks, grouped by the job they do (judging the business, buying and holding, judging people and yourself). "Sources" counts distinct sources that use the framework or a clear synonym. Fisher talks about public stocks; each framework says how the seat translates it to a founder's decision, and where that is a stretch. Sources S003 to S008 are second-hand; a framework that rests only on them says so. Ideas tagged (K) are recalled, not sourced, and are never quoted.

---

## Judging the business

### 1. Scuttlebutt *(Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits)*
- **What it is:** Learn what a company is really like by asking the people around it, not the company: employees, former employees, customers, suppliers, competitors. Judge whether the answers carry real conviction.
- **Parts:** Ask questions and use judgment (S002). Managements guess what you want to hear (S002). Learn from people with direct familiarity with the company (S008, second-hand). The book gives it a short chapter (S005, second-hand).
- **When to use:** Before backing a team, a market, or a hire. For a founder: call five customers, two former employees of a rival, and one supplier before signing.
- **How to score:** 8 to 10 if the founder has already done the outside calls and can say what they heard. 4 to 6 if all the evidence comes from the team's own pitch. 1 to 3 if nobody outside has been asked.
- **Quote:** "The only way I know is to stick my long nose in and ask questions." (S002)
- **Sources:** 4 (S002, S003, S005, S008).

### 2. The fifteen points *(Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits)*
- **What it is:** A checklist of fifteen questions about market potential, new products, research, sales, margins, people, management depth, costs, financing, and honesty. A company need not pass every one, but it should pass most (S005, second-hand).
- **Parts:** Sales potential for years; management's drive to develop more products; research that works for its size; an above-average sales organization; a worthwhile profit margin and a plan to keep it; labor, executive, and personnel relations; depth of management; cost analysis and accounting controls; industry-specific clues; short or long outlook on profit; whether growth needs equity that dilutes holders; whether management talks freely only in good times; integrity (S004).
- **When to use:** As the structure for any "is this team and business worth a long bet" review.
- **How to score:** Count the points the evidence passes. 12 or more with integrity clear is an 8 to 10. Failing integrity caps the score at 2 whatever else passes (S004, S008, S005).
- **Quote:** "Does the company have a management of unquestionable integrity?" (S004)
- **Sources:** 3 (S004, S005, S006).

### 3. Management first: integrity and ability
- **What it is:** The people decide the outcome. Two traits are essential: business ability and integrity, shown as a real sense of trusteeship for the owners (S008, second-hand). You cannot know a management until you have lived with it.
- **Parts:** Ability: plans for what others barely see (S002), strong planning and quality (S001). Integrity: flat standard, no exceptions (S004, S005). Candor: do they talk in bad times too (S004). Treatment of staff: no repeated layoffs to flatter earnings (S002).
- **When to use:** Hiring a senior leader, picking a partner, backing a co-founder.
- **How to score:** Rate integrity and ability separately. A high ability with doubtful integrity scores 2 or less. Both high scores 8 or more.
- **Quote:** "Getting to know the management of a company is like getting married." (S001)
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S002, S004, S005, S008).

### 4. Dissent and tomorrow's story
- **What it is:** Good management is not the fashionable practices everyone has; that is yesterday's story. Look for people working on things others barely notice, and for a culture that lets people criticize management.
- **Parts:** Who is working on what is just starting to have potential (S002). Welcomes dissent (S002). What management boasts about is old news (S002).
- **When to use:** Judging whether a team will still lead in five years, not whether it is good today.
- **How to score:** High when a specific new effort exists and criticism is visibly allowed. Low when the pitch is a list of current best practices.
- **Quote:** "I want companies that welcome dissent, rather than stifle it, that don't penalize people who criticize what management is doing." (S002)
- **Sources:** 1 (S002).

### 5. The growth runway and the moat
- **What it is:** Look for companies with disciplined plans for dramatic long-range growth in profits, and with qualities that make it hard for newcomers to share in that growth. The biggest rewards go to those that outgrow their industry in both sales and profits (S005, second-hand).
- **Parts:** Products with sufficient market potential for several years; management that keeps developing new ones (S004). Low-cost producer, a world leader or able to match the best rival (S001). A rival-proof reason (S004).
- **When to use:** Any bet whose payoff depends on growth over many years.
- **How to score:** 8 to 10 for a clear runway with a stated reason a rival cannot copy. 4 to 6 for growth that depends on a fashionable market. 1 to 3 for no stated reason it lasts.
- **Quote:** "Buy into companies that have disciplined plans for achieving dramatic long-range growth in profits and that have inherent qualities making it difficult for newcomers to share in that growth." (S004)
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S004, S005, S007, S008).

### 6. New-product years hide growth
- **What it is:** When new products launch, marketing money and early production on the learning curve cost more than research does. Earnings can flatten for years while the company is in fact growing, and the market punishes it.
- **Parts:** Research is important, but marketing and early production cost more (S001). A string of launches bunched together flattens earnings (S001). Wall Street's short outlook reads this as a stall (S001).
- **When to use:** Judging a business whose margins dipped because it is investing in launches. For a founder: a down quarter after a product launch is not by itself a warning.
- **How to score:** Raise the score when the dip has a named cause (launches, a new plant) and an end date. Lower it when the dip has no cause the team can name.
- **Quote:** "Now people who are interested in growth but who aren't very sophisticated tend to measure it by how much you spend on R&D." (S001)
- **Sources:** 2 (S001, S005).

## Buying and holding

### 7. Quality at a fair price over cheap
- **What it is:** There are two approaches. One finds something so cheap it cannot fall much. His own finds something so good, if you do not overpay, that it grows a great deal. The bargain method is so well known that everyone has picked up what meets its formula.
- **Parts:** Graham's safeguards against decline (S001). Fisher: very large growth (S001). Bargains are rare while great growers gain by hundreds of percent each decade (S005, second-hand). A mediocre company cannot be a smart long-term buy at any price (S007, second-hand).
- **When to use:** Choosing between the cheap option and the best option.
- **How to score:** High when the better business is available at a price that does not need a miracle. Low when the case is only that it looks cheap.
- **Quote:** "Then there is my approach, which is to find something so good" (S001)
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S005, S007, S008).

### 8. Few, big positions
- **What it is:** Concentrate. Most of the real gains came from a very small number of stocks over decades. Do not hold a long list.
- **Parts:** 14 big winners from the 1930s to 1987, 2 of them in the 1930s, each held 8 or 9 to 30 years (S001). Four core stocks held about two-thirds of the money at the time of the interview (S001). Over twenty holdings is a sign of financial incompetence for an individual (S008, second-hand quote). A long list shows a lack of confidence (S006, second-hand).
- **When to use:** Deciding how many bets to run at once. For a founder: one core business, a few feeders, no long tail of side projects.
- **How to score:** High when capital and time go to the best one or two. Low when it is spread over many things the founder cannot follow.
- **Quote:** "I don't want to spend my time trying to earn a lot of little profits. I want very, very big profits that I'm ready to wait for." (S001)
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S003, S006, S008).

### 9. Hold through; sell almost never
- **What it is:** If the job was done right at purchase, the time to sell is almost never. There are only a few reasons to sell: the original buy was a mistake, the company no longer passes the points it was chosen on, or a clearly better use of the money exists.
- **Parts:** Do not sell because the price rose (S003). Sell if management or the basic situation has deteriorated, or if a deep conviction has not performed after three years (S003). Fisher's early lesson: his profits in one machinery stock dwarfed his in-and-out trading of a canner (S001).
- **When to use:** Deciding when to stop, sell, or shut a project.
- **How to score:** High when the plan names the facts that would end it, and ignores price noise. Low when the plan would stop on a bad quarter with no change in the business.
- **Quote:** "If you are in the right companies, the potential rise can be so enormous that everything else is secondary." (S003)
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S003, S006, S007, S008).

### 10. Do not time the market; do not buy the crowd's favorite
- **What it is:** Timing is damnably difficult. If a position is not obviously right, hedge. Avoid stocks everyone wants.
- **Parts:** No view on when a crash comes (S001). Keep 20% to 25% in cash and Treasury bills when unsure (S001). Standing room only at a meeting is a warning sign (S001). When clients want to buy five times what he proposed, it is usually too late (S001). Buying and holding beats timing (S005, second-hand).
- **When to use:** Deciding when to commit and how to react to a hot market.
- **How to score:** High when the plan works without a call on the market and holds back when everyone is eager. Low when it only works if timing is right.
- **Quote:** "I haven't the faintest idea whether we are in 1927 or 1929." (S001)
- **Sources:** 3 (S001, S005, S008).

### 11. Admit mistakes fast, let winners run
- **What it is:** Mistakes are part of the business. Take small losses early and let the best grow. More money has been lost waiting to break even than for any other reason.
- **Parts:** Losses up to 50% in two cases; many +/-10% (S001). The "get your bait back" argument is only psychological comfort (S001). Do not just take profits for the satisfaction (S008, second-hand). Waiting to come out even is the costliest habit (S006, second-hand).
- **When to use:** Reviewing a bet that is underwater, or a winner someone wants to trim.
- **How to score:** High when the plan sets a time to admit a miss and does not trim winners on emotion. Low when it averages down with no new reason or sells winners to feel safe.
- **Quote:** "Either this is a better investment than another one or a worse one." (S001)
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S003, S006, S008).

## Judging people and yourself

### 12. Stay in your circle
- **What it is:** Know a field well and do not try to be a jack of all trades. Fisher kept to manufacturing companies that use natural science to expand markets and said he was more qualified there.
- **Parts:** The weakness of many approaches is trying to master everything (S001). Retailing and finance offer excellent chances, but he felt less qualified (S001). Understand what you buy, and apply judgment after a thorough review (S008, second-hand).
- **When to use:** A founder stepping into a market or skill they do not own.
- **How to score:** High in the founder's home field. Low outside it, unless the plan buys the knowledge first.
- **Quote:** "I think a weakness of many people's approach to investment is that they try to be jacks of all trades and masters of none." (S001)
- **Sources:** 2 (S001, S008).

### 13. Judge advisers by their record and a three-year clock
- **What it is:** To pick a manager, ask for a transcript of what they actually did. Give a gold star to those who take small losses fast and let profits run; avoid those who do the reverse. He told clients to judge him over three years.
- **Parts:** Transcript of actual trades (S001). Three-year test for himself and for holdings (S008, second-hand).
- **When to use:** Choosing an adviser, agency, or manager.
- **How to score:** High when there is a written record that matches the claims. Low when the record is a story.
- **Quote:** "If they take their profits quickly and let their losses run, don't go near them." (S001)
- **Sources:** 2 (S001, S008).

### 14. Conservative means keeping buying power at low risk
- **What it is:** A conservative investment is one most likely to keep purchasing power at a minimum of risk. He rates risk by quality and valuation: the least risky is a company strong on marketing, people, and uniqueness that the market does not yet rate highly. A strong company that is highly rated suits holding more than buying.
- **Parts:** Four dimensions: marketing and production skill, people, uniqueness, valuation (S007, second-hand). Debt raises risk because fixed interest adds stress (S007, S002).
- **When to use:** Setting how much of the founder's runway a bet can take.
- **How to score:** High for strong, unleveraged businesses. Low for leveraged, mediocre ones.
- **Quote:** "And I don't like highly leveraged companies, even if they are well run." (S002)
- **Sources:** 2 (S002, S007).
