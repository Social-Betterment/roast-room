# Stories: Philip Fisher

9 stories and case studies he tells, most useful first. Figures are as he tells them or as the source reports them. All are from the fetched sources; S003 to S006 are second-hand.

1. **Motorola, held for decades.** He tells clients that $1,000 put in the stock in 1957 had become $1,993,846 after all the ups and downs. He says that if he had sold on a worry that it was overpriced 10 or 15 years before, he would not have known when to get back in. Lesson: price alone is not a reason to sell a great business. (S003, quoting Forbes 1996; S001 for Motorola as a core holding and its leader's standards)

2. **Texas Instruments and getting your bait back.** He bought a block at $14 for a client trust. At $28 the trustees asked to sell half to get their bait back, and again at $35. The stock later went above $250, then fell to the mid-50s, never back to $35. Lesson: "getting your bait back" is comfort, not analysis. (S001)

3. **Food Machinery and California Packing.** In the 1930s he started his business on Food Machinery. Three times he bought and sold a canner at lows and highs. By 1940 or 1941 he found he had spent more time on the trades than on watching the machinery maker, whose profits already dwarfed the trading gains. Lesson: buy low and sell high and bank the gain "isn't valid"; stay with the best. (S001)

4. **Fourteen big winners.** Over about five decades he found 14 stocks that returned at least seven times his money, up to thousands of times, held 8 or 9 to 30 years. He bought three to four times as many others that made more than they lost, and had two losses as high as 50%. Lesson: the weeding costs, and the big ones pay for everything. (S001)

5. **Raychem and the flat years.** A company that had grown earnings steadily had flat earnings for several years while it launched a bunch of new products. Wall Street doubted it. He saw it as the epitome of a growth company. Lesson: read the cause of a dip. (S001)

6. **Hyperinflation after World War I.** To prepare for what he expected in 1987, he studied France and Germany after the war. Holders of the very best stocks were uncomfortable throughout but ended with about 80% of their purchasing power. Lesson: pick the right stocks and hold some cash when unsure. (S001)

7. **When clients want more.** When clients agreed with reluctance he was more often right than when they asked to buy five times what he proposed, which he took as a warning that it was too late. Lesson: enthusiasm is a signal to slow down. (S001)

8. **The $50 quibble.** In the book, an investor refused to pay an extra $50 on 100 shares and later missed $46,500 in gains. Lesson: do not haggle over eighths when the move is large. (S006, second-hand)

9. **What Buffett took from him.** In an introduction to the 1987 interview, Warren Buffett says he sought Fisher out in the early 1960s after reading his books and learned the "scuttlebutt" approach: talk to competitors, suppliers, and customers. This is Buffett's account, not Fisher's. (S001)
