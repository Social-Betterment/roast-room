# Stories: Mohnish Pabrai

14 stories and case studies he tells, most useful first. Figures are as he tells them; they sometimes vary between tellings. All are from the transcripts in this brain.

1. **His father, the astrologer, and the rope.** His father was an engineer-turned-entrepreneur with good ideas who used heavy leverage and went bankrupt several times; by the time Pabrai was 11 or 12 it was the ninth time. An astrologer came on Sundays and painted a good future. His father said he was at the bottom of a deep well and needed a rope. Lesson: leverage makes the first storm fatal, and people need a story to hold onto in a crisis. (S017)

2. **The financial crisis.** His portfolio fell 67 percent, from about $600 million to about $200 million. He built a spreadsheet of what he thought the companies were worth and meditated on that number as his rope. Lesson: he is open about his own drawdown and about using a model as comfort, which is why he later says to drop Excel. (S017)

3. **The Turkish warehouse landlord.** On a 2019 trip a friend took him to a company with a $15 million market cap and roughly $800 million of liquidation value. It rented warehouses to Amazon, IKEA, and Carrefour on inflation-indexed leases, with about $200 million of debt. He bought about a third of it for $8 million and kept adding; he now owns over 40 percent and says the market cap is about $1.5 billion. Lesson: look where nobody is looking, then check the simple facts. (S017)

4. **IPSCO and CONSOL.** In 2004 he bought a Canadian steel company with $15 a share in cash on its balance sheet that he could explain in three sentences to a 10-year-old. Years later a post by David Einhorn about CONSOL Energy looked like the same setup, and he cloned it. Lesson: a clean past win is a template for the next bet. (S016, S017, S019)

5. **Nick Sleep returns the money.** When a regulator called a $2.5 billion fund non-diversified, Nick Sleep and his partner returned outside capital and told investors to put one third each in Amazon, Berkshire, and Costco. Amazon grew until it was about 80 percent. Lesson: a few great businesses held for a long time beat a diversified book. (S017)

6. **The billionaire who stayed within a mile.** John Arrillaga invested only in real estate within about one mile of Stanford and knew every building's rent history. Lesson: the circle can be tiny if you are at its center. (S017)

7. **Walmart in a failed Nifty 50.** He imagines a 1970 portfolio with 2 percent in Walmart and the other 98 percent going to zero; it would still beat the S&P 500. Lesson: one winner left alone can cover a 98 percent error rate. (S017)

8. **Sam Walton and the candle display.** After touring a messy competitor's store with his managers, Walton said that the candle display was great. Lesson: copy the best part of everything, even from a poor operator. (S015)

9. **Costco and the Levi's closeout.** Costco bought a batch of jeans cheaply and could have priced them far below retail, but its founder kept to the 15 percent markup cap to protect trust. Lesson: trust is built from many such choices. (S015)

10. **Slide 10.** Before leaving his job for his third startup, he pitched a bank CIO who stopped him at slide 10 and refused to look at any other. Other buyers cared about the same pain. He rebuilt the deck around it and got a purchase order about 15 minutes after the next pitch. Lesson: customers tell you what to build. (S015)

11. **The Buffett lunch and the margin calls.** At a lunch Buffett told how a friend's Berkshire shares were sold under margin calls at about $40 a share; they are now worth over $700,000 each. The takeaway Buffett gave was to spend less than you earn and use no leverage. Lesson: leverage forces a sale at the worst time. (S006, S007, S020)

12. **"Fast is slow."** His friend Duan Yongping, founder of Oppo and Vivo, once went into a board meeting, said those three words, and left. The team spent months working out what it meant and then beat its competitors. Lesson: slowing down and doing fewer things well is faster. (S010, S015)

13. **The ones he sold too early.** He says his fund once owned about 1 percent of Ferrari bought for about $10 million, invested in Progressive about 25 years ago and says he should have left it alone, and sold Goldman Sachs, which he bought at $65 (Buffett's price was $130). Lesson: do not sell a rare great business. (S017)

14. **Dexter Shoes.** He points to Buffett's purchase of Dexter Shoes as the kind of mistake his checklist now asks about. Lesson: learn from the best investors' misses, not only their hits. (S017)
