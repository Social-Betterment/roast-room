# Stories: Peter Lynch

13 stories and case studies he tells, most useful first. Figures are as he tells them; they sometimes vary between tellings. All are from the fetched sources.

1. **L'eggs pantyhose and the No Nonsense check.** His wife brought home L'eggs, sold at the supermarket where women shop weekly rather than in department stores. He researched it and made Hanes his biggest position. When a rival put No Nonsense right beside it, he bought 48 pairs, gave them to colleagues to try, and they said it was not as good, so he held on. Lesson: a hunch from daily life is the start of the research, and you test the rival yourself. (S001, S002)

2. **Walmart: you do not have to be first.** Walmart went public in 1970 with 37 stores. Ten years later profits and the stock were up about twenty-fold, and an investor who waited could still make large multiples because the company was in a small share of the country. Lesson: check how much room is left, not how old the story is. (S001, S004, S005)

3. **McDonald's overseas.** People said McDonald's was done in the US; he asked how many countries it was in, saw 20-store beginnings in France, Germany, and Spain on the way to hundreds, and held it. Lesson: ask what inning it is. (S002, S005)

4. **Chrysler in 1982 and 1990.** He recommended Chrysler on Wall Street Week at about $10 when relatives said it was going bankrupt. It had cash for three years. He recommended it again in 1990 at $10 after a split. Lesson: check solvency, then ignore the headlines. (S001, S002)

5. **The Crash of 1987.** He left on vacation to Ireland, the market fell, he went home early, and in two days the fund lost about a third. There was little he could do. Lesson: expect large declines and decide in advance what you will do. (S001)

6. **1990 was scarier than 1987.** Iraq invaded Kuwait, major banks were in trouble, and a recession hit; he says it took faith in the country's future. Lesson: the fundamentals in 1987 were fine, in 1990 they were not, and you have to tell the difference. (S001)

7. **Taco Bell.** The first stock he bought at Magellan. Fidelity's trade room asked what Taco Bell was. Pepsi bought it at $30 when he held a big position at $18, and he says it could have been much higher. Lesson: a good small business can be bought out before the story finishes. (S002)

8. **Barbra Streisand's call.** She owned stocks and could not sleep. He asked her to name five she owned and what they did. She could not say what the companies did. Lesson: if you do not understand what you own, you will not hold through a drop. (S002)

9. **Missing Apple and Starbucks.** His daughters gave him an iPod. Apple had a low price and cash and was making $150 on a $200 device, and he did not look. He says he missed Starbucks too ("Brain cramp"). Lesson: the edge is wasted if you do not use it. (S002, S005)

10. **The weeds and flowers line and Buffett.** Warren Buffett phoned him about using his line about cutting winners and holding losers being like watering the weeds and cutting the flowers, and said his name would be mud in Nebraska if he did not visit. Lesson: the line is widely quoted; use his own words. (S002)

11. **Caddy to Fidelity.** He caddied from age 11, heard members talk about stocks, and caddied for the Fidelity president. He got the job after one summer in 1966 with 75 applicants for three jobs. Lesson: he credits an odd route and relationships. (S001, S002)

12. **Flying Tiger, the first ten-bagger.** In college he studied air freight, put $1,000 into Flying Tiger, and the Vietnam War lifted the stock about ten-fold. He sold along the way and says it helped pay for graduate school. Lesson: a few big winners are enough. (S001)

13. **Retiring at 46.** He left Magellan in 1990 because of the hours and family, and says his father died at 46. In 2025 he says he turned down offers of a closed-end fund. Lesson: he treats his own time as a budget. (S001, S002)

## Numbers he cites about his own record

- He took Magellan at $20 million in May 1977 and a $1,000 stake on that day was worth $28,000 by 31 May 1990 (S001).
- One in every hundred Americans was in Magellan (S002).
- The fund fell 10 to 30 percent eight times in 12 years (S003).
- He was right about six in ten stock picks, "six and a half" in later tellings (S001, S005, S002).
